    Each name

The tranches follow one rule for every name: buy 1 is today's price, and buys 2 and 3 sit at the dips that an asset with this volatility reaches in about two in three and one in three six-month periods.     Take-profit levels are multiples of today's price, so they are fixed prices you could place as resting orders. Both plans use the same ones.

    Plan A · Spot ladder

    No leverage, no liquidation, no funding. Buys more as price falls; the stop ends the plan if price goes one step below the last buy.

    Plan B · Isolated futures at 2× and 3×

    The whole allocation is posted as margin on one long per name, opened at today's price. There is no ladder: each add would move the average entry and the liquidation price, and the stop only works if it sits above liquidation.

    • A stop is always kept at least 5 points above the liquidation price. When the rule you chose would sit lower, it is pulled up and marked.
    • The loss shown at the stop assumes it fills at the stop price. In a fast fall or a weekend gap it fills lower.
    • Funding is charged on the open position every 8 hours. 0.01% is the usual baseline when longs pay; it can be lower or negative in a falling market and several times higher in a hot one.

    The plans side by side in 2,000 simulated years

    Each run walks the three prices forward 365 days using their own last-year volatility and their measured correlation. Plan A fills a rung when price closes at or below it; both plans sell at each take-profit when price closes at or above it and close everything when the stop is touched. The only thing that changes between rows is the assumed market direction. This is a calculator for comparing plans under an assumption, not a forecast.

    Spot or isolated futures

    An isolated long is closed by the exchange when price falls by roughly one divided by the leverage. The table counts how often each name fell that far within six months of a random day.

    • A buy ladder and leverage pull against each other. At 3× the position is liquidated near −31%, which is above the last buy for every coin here, so that tranche would never be reached with the first position still alive.
    • Perpetual futures also pay or receive funding every eight hours, and a 6 to 12 month hold pays it roughly a thousand times. Spot has no funding and cannot be liquidated.
    • Liquidation distance uses a 2% maintenance margin; the exact figure varies by exchange and position size.

    Method, limits and sources

    • Prices. Coins: Binance spot daily candles, from each listing. Stocks: Yahoo Finance daily, ten years (SanDisk only since its February 2025 listing). Everything is as of the last completed daily close.
    • Buy tranches. For a price with no trend and annual volatility σ, the chance of touching a dip of depth d within six months is 2·Φ(ln(1−d) / (σ·√0.5)). Buys 2 and 3 solve that for two in three (67%) and one in three (33%), using the last year's daily volatility. The "history" columns are plain counts over every six-month window in the asset's own record.
    • Upside odds. "Touched" means the daily high reached the level at any point inside the next 12 months. "Still there" means the close 12 months later was at or above it. The gap between the two is the case for resting sell orders.
    • Stops. The spot stop is the dip reached in about 15% of six-month periods by the same volatility formula, one step under the last buy. The isolated stop follows the rule you choose and is never closer than 5 points to liquidation. "Stop first / 2× first / neither" counts, for every day in the record, which of the two prices was touched first in the following 12 months; a day on which both were touched counts as the stop.
    • Isolated futures. Liquidation distance is 1 ÷ leverage minus a 2% maintenance margin (about −48% at 2×, −31% at 3×); your exchange's figure depends on the contract and position size. Funding is taken on the entry value of whatever is still open.
    • Adding on strength. A reclaim is the first close above the average after at least 20 trading days below it (50-day) or 30 (200-day); a new 90-day high counts when there was none in the 60 days before. Results are pooled across the group and measured from that day's close.
    • Small samples. The coin history covers about six years, which holds two bear markets. Days inside one bear market are not independent, so a figure built on 800 days may rest on two or three real episodes. Sui and Arbitrum have 3.5 years. SanDisk has under two, all of it one boom, so its history columns are left blank.
    • The simulation assumes volatility and correlation stay at last year's levels and that prices have no memory. Real markets trend and gap, and in sell-offs correlations rise, so real bad cases are worse than the simulated ones.
    • Tokenized stocks. Some exchanges list tokenized versions of the three stocks. The levels here are from the US listings; a tokenized version trades around the clock and can sit at a small premium or discount.
